Exhibit 003 — The same event, seen differently

What does spacetime look like
when you watch it collapse?

6M

At six times the total mass the inspiral ends: there are no stable orbits below the innermost stable circular orbit, so the star stops orbiting and starts falling. One and three quarter final revolutions along a plunge geodesic, while the hole's tidal field stretches it from 5% to nearly 50% elongation — the star you see is an ellipsoid, pointed at the hole, and the ray marcher intersects it as one. Then the horizon swallows it and grows by 23%.

Same event

Same neutron star and black hole as exhibit 001 — same masses, same Peters-formula decay, same merger. What's different is how the light gets computed. Here every pixel traces an actual photon path through the Schwarzschild metric. In exhibit 001 the lensing was a screen-space approximation; here it's real integrated geometry.

Per pixel

Every pixel integrates the geodesic equation until its photon escapes, lands on the star, or crosses the horizon. The step size holds the angular progress around the hole constant rather than the distance travelled, which is what an orbit actually needs: rays winding close to the photon ring get the resolution they require and distant rays stop paying for it. Typical cost is 55 steps, worst case 228, against a budget of 256. The distorted starfield behind it all is real — NASA's Deep Star Maps 2020, 1.7 billion stars from Hipparcos-2, Tycho-2 and Gaia DR2, projected onto a sphere.

The shadow

The black disk isn't the event horizon — it's bigger. Any photon passing within b_crit = √27 × M never escapes. Earlier versions of this exhibit tested that analytically before marching, which sounds more rigorous than it is: the flat-space impact parameter measured from a camera sixteen Schwarzschild radii out sits about 1.2% inside where the integration actually captures, so the shortcut and the ray marcher disagreed at the edge. Now capture is decided by the same integration that draws everything else, and the fragments straddling the boundary are supersampled. The bright arc around it is the photon ring: light that nearly completed an orbit before escaping toward you, carrying a squeezed image of the entire sky.

Disappearance

When the neutron star crosses behind the shadow it doesn't simply disappear. Light from its surface wraps around the hole and arrives as arcs on both sides, closing into a near-complete Einstein ring at the moment of alignment — not added as an effect, just what the integrated paths do. Watch which side of the ring is brighter, too: the star orbits at roughly a third of the speed of light, so the limb coming toward you is beamed and blueshifted while the receding one is dimmed and reddened, a contrast of about fourteen to one.
