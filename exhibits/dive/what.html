The physics, somewhat explained

So, what are you actually looking at?

A fall into a black hole, modelled after Sagittarius A*, all the way through the horizon and down to where the equations stop working. Some of what you see is computed. Some of it is drawn. This page is about which is which, and why the line falls where it does — including why this exhibit stops instead of taking you through to another universe.

01

The camera has mass

In the other exhibits, the camera is a thing that hovers. It sits at a fixed distance, does not fall, and generally behaves like a tripod ... that also happens to disregard gravity. That's fine when the point is to look at a black hole. It stops being fine when the point is to get in there.

So this exhibit works differently. The camera is modelled after a particle with mass, released twelve horizon-radii out with slightly less angular momentum than a stable orbit needs, and then integrated through the Kerr metric until it can't be integrated anymore. All that to say; the math and physics decide the journey.

Math time. The shader integrates photon paths using a Hamiltonian:

H = ½ g^μν p_μ p_ν
g^μν — the inverse metric, which is to say, the shape of spacetime here
p_μ — the four-momentum of whatever is moving
H — zero for light. Minus one half for anything with mass.

That's the whole difference. A photon is a solution with H = 0. An observer is a solution with H = −½. Same equation, same RK4 integrator, one different number on the right-hand side. The camera and the light it sees are running the same physics against each other.

One more thing falls out of having a camera with mass, and it turns out to be the thing that makes the second half of this exhibit possible at all. A falling observer carries four directions with them: one pointing along their own future, and three pointing right, up, and forward. Together they form what's called an orthonormal tetrad, four rigid rulers carried through a space that is anything but rigid.
Building it is ordinary linear algebra, Gram-Schmidt on four vectors, with the single oddity that the time direction has length minus one instead of plus one. The exhibit rebuilds it every frame from the camera's own momentum, and checks it: ten dot products that should come out as minus one, one, one, one and zeros everywhere else. They hold to eleven decimal places all the way down.

Why it matters is that the tetrad is what turns a pixel into a photon. A pixel is a direction in someone's eye, and "someone's eye" is exactly what a tetrad is. Without one you have to guess at a correction factor for aberration and bolt it on afterwards. With one, aberration naturally arises from the arithmetic.

The Hamiltonian is also a free correctness check, which is the real reason to build it this way. H is supposed to stay at exactly −½ forever. If it drifts, the integration is wrong. During development the step size was tuned by watching that number, and it's still on screen: it's the fourth readout in the bottom bar. It holds to six decimal places for the entire journey down through the horizon, and then, in the last stretch, it starts to move. Chapter 07 is about why that isn't a bug.

02

Two clocks, two completely different stories

Watch the readouts as the fall goes in. Proper time, the clock the falling observer carries, ticks along at a completely ordinary rate. About twenty minutes, start to finish, and nothing about it feels unusual from the inside. Coordinate time, the clock of someone watching safely from a distance, does something else entirely. It climbs, then climbs faster, then runs away to infinity as the horizon approaches.

Both are correct. The distant observer never sees the fall complete. They watch the image slow down, redden, dim, and freeze just short of the boundary, fading toward nothing but never quite arriving. Meanwhile the person actually falling crosses the horizon at a specific moment on their own watch and notices nothing at all.

This is where the old textbook version of black holes gets stuck, and it's worth knowing why. The original Schwarzschild coordinates blow up at the horizon: terms divide by zero, and the mathematics appears to say that nothing can ever get through. It took until 1958, more than forty years after the solution was written down, for David Finkelstein to make it properly clear that this was a failure of the coordinates rather than of the spacetime. Nothing is torn at the horizon. The map was just badly drawn there.

This exhibit uses Kerr-Schild coordinates, which stay perfectly finite across the boundary. That's the entire reason the camera can keep going. There's no special-casing at the horizon in the code, no handoff, no seam. The integrator doesn't even notice it's happened.

Which creates a small trap worth admitting to, because this exhibit fell into it. Kerr-Schild coordinates come with their own time coordinate, and being regular at the horizon is exactly the property they were built for — so it doesn't diverge either. For a while the readout displayed that number and simply printed an infinity symbol once the crossing happened, which looked right and was theatre. The clock that actually runs away belongs to Boyer-Lindquist coordinates, and getting it means undoing the transformation between them along the whole path:

dt_BL = dt_KS − (2Mr / Δ) dr
Δ — equals r² − 2Mr + a², and has a root exactly at the horizon
the integral — therefore diverges logarithmically on the way in
inside — Δ changes sign and the exterior clock stops existing altogether

That's the number in the panel now, and it climbs on its own. Two coordinate clocks describing one fall: the one being integrated stays finite, and the one being displayed runs to infinity. Both are correct, and the difference between them is the whole content of "from the outside, it never finishes."

Hold on to that distinction, because chapter 05 is about a surface where the coordinates behave, the equations stay finite, and the spacetime fails anyway. Telling those two kinds of failure apart is most of the work.

03

Falling makes the black hole look smaller

This is the one that catches people out, and it's genuinely counterintuitive rather than just surprising.

You'd expect the shadow to grow as you approach, swelling until it swallows everything and the screen goes dark. It doesn't. The shadow gets smaller on the way in, and the sky bunches up into a bright, compressed cone ahead of you.

The reason is speed. By the time the camera reaches the horizon it's moving at roughly 65% of light speed relative to a hovering observer at the same spot, and at those speeds relativistic aberration reshapes the entire sky. Light coming from ahead gets swept forward and concentrated. Light from behind gets stretched out and thinned. Running headfirst into a black hole does the same thing to your view of the sky that running headfirst into a rainstorm does to your view of the rain.

🌧️
Stand still in vertical rain and it falls straight down. Run, and it seems to slant toward your face, the faster the steeper. The rain didn't change direction. You did. Light behaves the same way, except the effect is on angle and brightness and colour all at once, and near a black hole you're moving fast enough for it to dominate what you see.

The gravitational bending is still there and still enormous. It's simply that aberration pulls the other way and, on the approach, wins. Put those together and falling in doesn't look like being engulfed by darkness at all. It looks more like the universe folding shut behind you.

04

Inside, the radius becomes a moment in time

Crossing the horizon changes something structural about the geometry, and it's stranger than "gravity gets stronger."

Outside, r is a spatial direction. You can move toward smaller r, or toward larger r, or hold still, the same way you can walk north or south or stand where you are. Inside, r stops being a direction you can choose and becomes something more like a date on a calendar. Decreasing r is as unavoidable as next Tuesday. Not because something is pulling hard enough to overwhelm you, but because "outward" has stopped being one of the available directions in the same way that "last week" isn't.

That's what the phrase "point of no return" is actually pointing at, and it's a much stranger claim than the usual telling suggests. There's no wall, no barrier, no force to fight. The singularity isn't a place waiting in front of you. It's an event in your future.

There's a standard objection at this point, and for a long time this exhibit believed it. Every other exhibit here works by tracing light backward from a stationary observer's eye, and inside the horizon there is no such thing as a stationary observer. So the interior got drawn by hand instead.

The objection is half right. Stationary observers really do stop existing at the horizon. But nothing ever required the observer to be stationary, only to exist, and a falling one exists all the way down and carries a perfectly good tetrad with them. Trace backward from that, in coordinates that stay finite across the boundary, and the rays behave. The interior is now computed on the same footing as everything else.

What comes back is worth describing, because it is not what the drawn version showed and it is not what the popular telling predicts either.

Inside, the sky splits in two along a sharp edge, and the test for which side a pixel lands on is a single number. Every photon carries an energy measured at infinity, and that number is conserved along its whole path. Light that actually came from the outside universe has it positive, because there is no negative-energy light out there to send. Trace a ray backward from the falling observer's eye, read the number off, and if it is negative that ray never touched the exterior. In the fully extended solution it arrives from a white hole, which a black hole formed by collapsing matter does not have. The shader can therefore decide before the ray takes a single step.

Where the sign flips is a cone, and its half-angle has a closed form:

cos θ = A / √(A² − 1 + f)
A — the observer's own conserved energy, close to one for a fall that started far out
f — the Kerr-Schild potential, 2M/r in the equatorial plane
θ — the angular radius of the dark region, measured from the direction of fall

A cone exists only once f passes one, which happens at the ergosphere rather than at the horizon. From there it opens steadily. By r = 0.34 Rs it is about 54° across from its axis, and as r runs down it tends toward 90° without ever reaching it.

So the image most people carry, of the universe closing to a shrinking porthole that finally winks out, is wrong. The dark cone sits in the direction of fall and takes less than a third of the sky by the time the equations quit. The rest is still the outside universe, and it is still wider than the field of view. Turn the camera sideways this deep in and you get stars, not a wall.

The cone is also the exhibit's clock

There was an embarrassment sitting in this exhibit for a long time, and it's worth naming because the fix is the interesting part. The whole thesis is that crossing the horizon is uneventful. Meanwhile the crossing was, by a wide margin, the most eventful thing on screen: the camera swung 180° over three and a half seconds, the drawn interior faded in, a label announced what had just happened, and a threshold deep in the ray marcher moved.

None of that was physics. The ray marching is genuinely identical on both sides — same integrator, same coordinates, no branch anywhere that mentions the horizon. Every one of those four things was an authored decision that had been keyed to the crossing because the crossing was the obvious place to key things to.

They're keyed to the cone angle now, and the cone is very deliberately not a horizon phenomenon. It needs f > 1, which happens at the ergosphere, 31% further out. Measured along this exact trajectory:

θ(r)
r = 1.000 Rs — the ergosphere. θ = 0.4°, the cone is just being born
r = 0.900 Rs — θ = 19.3°
r = 0.763 Rs — the horizon. θ = 30.3°, and nothing marks it
r = 0.600 Rs — θ = 40.6°
r = 0.275 Rs — θ = 59.4°, where the integration halts

There is no feature at 30.3°. So the turnaround now takes about fourteen seconds instead of three and a half, begins well before the crossing, ends well after it, and the horizon passes somewhere in the middle of a slow continuous movement where nobody notices it.

The signal didn't get deleted, though — that would be its own kind of dishonesty, since you do need to know when you crossed for the claim to mean anything. It moved out of the image and into the instruments. One readout in the bottom bar changes colour as the radius passes 1.000 Rs. That's the entire announcement. The numbers are allowed to shout. The view isn't allowed to blink.

Which sets up the last question, the one everybody actually wants answered: what's at the bottom, and can you get through it?

05

The door that isn't there

Everyone knows black holes have a singularity at the centre, and everyone pictures a point. For a spinning one, that picture is wrong. Kerr's solution has a ring singularity: a circle sitting in the equatorial plane with radius a = J/Mc, which for this exhibit's spin of 0.85 works out to 0.85 M across. The centre of that ring is not itself singular. There's a hole in the middle of the hole.

And the mathematics is unambiguous about what's on the other side. Continue Kerr's solution through the ring and you reach a region where r is negative and gravity pushes instead of pulls. Keep going the other way, outward through the second horizon, and the extended solution opens into another exterior universe entirely — and then another, and another, an infinite chain of them. This isn't a fringe reading. It's what you get by writing the solution down and refusing to stop early.

An earlier version of this exhibit took that seriously enough to build it. The camera was lifted over the ring's plane, carried through the opening, and came out into a procedurally generated sky full of galaxies. It was the prettiest thing on the page. It's gone now, and this chapter is why.

Two horizons, not one

A rotating black hole has two. The outer one, r₊ = 7.63 in this exhibit's units, is the famous one — the point of no return from chapter 04. The inner one sits at:

r₋ = M − √(M² − a²) = 0.4732 M
M — the mass
a — the spin, 0.85 M here
r₋ — the Cauchy horizon, and the actual end of this story

Between the two, r behaves like time and the fall is compulsory. Cross the inner one and it flips back: r becomes a direction again, you can steer, and you can miss the ring entirely. That's what makes the passage sound survivable in the first place. Everything downstream — the ring, the negative-r region, the other universes — sits on the far side of that surface.

Why the inner horizon is not like the outer one

Chapter 02 made a point of the outer horizon being fine underneath a bad map. It's tempting to assume the same trick works twice. It doesn't, and the reason is not about coordinates at all.

Think about what arrives at the inner horizon. Not just you — everything that ever falls into this hole after you do, plus every photon from the outside universe that ever crosses the outer horizon, for the entire future history of that universe. All of it, arriving on one surface, compressed into a finite amount of your proper time.

📻
Imagine tuning into a radio broadcast that plays the rest of eternity, and the closer you get to a certain moment, the faster it runs. Not "sped up" as a figure of speech: infinite content delivered in finite time, which means infinite frequency, which means infinite energy. That's the blueshift at the inner horizon. Every wave that ever comes in stacks up on that surface.

Poisson and Israel worked out in 1990 what that does to the geometry, and the result has a name that says it well: mass inflation. The effective mass measured just inside the inner horizon grows exponentially. Curvature grows with it. The surface that was supposed to be a smooth doorway turns into a genuine singularity — not a coordinate artefact, an actual one, where the theory stops having anything to say.

So the two horizons fail in opposite directions. The outer one looked broken and turned out to be fine. The inner one looks fine — the metric is perfectly well-behaved there in the eternal, undisturbed Kerr solution — and turns out to be broken the moment you let anything else fall in. And things do fall in. That's how black holes are made.

This is still live research rather than a closed case. The current consensus is "strong cosmic censorship holds, in the sense that matters": the inner horizon does become singular, but the singularity is weak — tidal distortion stays finite even as curvature diverges, so an infalling object isn't necessarily torn apart by it. What's genuinely dead is the idea of continuing the solution smoothly past that surface. Whatever is on the other side, general relativity cannot tell you, because the equations no longer determine it. Getting an answer needs a theory of quantum gravity, and there isn't one yet.

And the wormhole?

Worth separating out, because it's a different objection with a different answer. The traversable wormhole — the one you could actually fly a ship through, worked out properly by Morris and Thorne in 1988 — has a problem that has nothing to do with black hole interiors. To hold a throat open you need something that pushes light apart. All ordinary matter does the opposite: gravity focuses, always, for anything with sensible energy conditions. You need negative energy density, in quantity, held stable, for as long as the throat exists.

Negative energy density does exist. The Casimir effect produces it, in amounts measured between plates a few nanometres apart, and quantum inequalities put hard limits on how much you can borrow and for how long. Scaling it up to hold a human-sized throat open lands you somewhere around a planet's worth of negative energy. That isn't an engineering gap. There's no known material that does this.

The original Einstein-Rosen bridge from 1935 fails differently and more simply. It's dynamic: the throat opens, pinches off, and closes faster than light can cross it. Send a photon in from each side and they can meet in the middle. Neither one comes out the other end. There's a connection in the geometry, but not one you can travel through.

So the Kerr passage never needed exotic matter — it's a real feature of a real vacuum solution. It just needed the inner horizon to hold. It doesn't.

06

The sky doesn't go dark. It runs out of colours.

The blueshift diverges. That was the last chapter's conclusion, and it's the reason the passage isn't there. This chapter is about what diverging blueshift actually does to a picture, because the honest answer is much stranger than the one this exhibit used to give.

For a long time the interior here was drawn. Procedural folds on a stereographic projection, a golden ring, a white curtain over the end. It looked good and it was invented. What finally settled it was a measurement rather than an argument: at a third of a horizon radius, 55% of the observer's sky is genuine integrated starfield, 24% is the dark cone, 21% is rays too redshifted to carry anything, and zero percent runs out of step budget. The ray tracer never gave up. The drawing was painted over the half of the frame that is black for two good physical reasons.

Blueshift moves the whole spectrum, not the colour

Here's the thing that's easy to state and hard to picture. Blueshift by a factor g doesn't tint light blue. It multiplies every frequency by g. An eye responds to roughly 400 to 700 nanometres and nothing else, so what it is actually looking at is the source's spectrum between 400/g and 700/g. Three consequences follow, and none of them is a design decision.

The stars go out long before the sky gets dark. At g around three, visible light has moved into the ultraviolet. The stars haven't dimmed — they've left the eye's bandwidth from the top. At the same moment, infrared is being pushed up into the visible from below, so a different set of sources switches on, in a different colour, with a different pattern on the sky.

Structure disappears before brightness does. The far-infrared sky is dust glow rather than point sources: smoother, more diffuse, fewer edges. And by the time the microwave background is the thing standing in the visible band, there is no pattern at all. The CMB is uniform to one part in a hundred thousand. Not dim. Not dark. Featureless, and extremely bright.

And the end of the sequence is daylight. A blackbody stays a blackbody under Doppler shift — it just gets hotter. The CMB sits at 2.725 K. Bring it into the visible and you have multiplied by roughly 1900, which puts it at about 5180 K. That is the colour of sunlight. The screen going white at the end of this exhibit is not a fade. It is the cosmic microwave background, blueshifted into the range your eyes happen to work in, arriving with no structure because it never had any.

Because g is largest right at the rim of the dark cone — that's where a photon's energy at infinity passes through zero — the whole sequence doesn't happen everywhere at once. It sweeps outward as concentric zones, each one a different part of the source spectrum standing in the visible, with the rim always furthest ahead. The order is set by the geometry, not by a timeline.

The same sky, over and over, thinner each time

There's a second thing in the interior image that has to be preserved rather than drawn. A ray that wraps once around the hole before escaping gives the primary image of the sky. Two wraps gives the secondary. Three, the tertiary — an infinite stack of them, packed tighter and tighter toward the shadow's edge.

The instinct is to make the higher ones fainter. That instinct is wrong, and the reason is one of the tidier results in relativity: specific intensity divided by frequency cubed is invariant along a light ray. The secondary image of the Milky Way has exactly the same surface brightness as the primary. It isn't dimmer. It's compressed.

Measured on this trajectory, the angular magnification in that band runs up to 680. A quarter of a degree of viewing direction can map to 150° of sky. So the multiple images are already in the render, and always were — they're just squeezed far below one pixel, where sampling them at a single point turns them into noise, and noise reads as nothing.

The fix isn't a drawing, it's arithmetic. The shader keeps a running total of how far each ray's direction has turned, which tells it how many images deep that pixel is, and samples the sky texture across the right area instead of at one point. That threshold had to be measured too: the baseline shifts a lot with depth — half the rays exceed 0.24 turns at 1.05 Rs and 0.84 turns at 0.30 Rs, because even a "direct" ray bends hard down there — so a fixed cutoff would have blurred two thirds of the sky at the bottom, which is precisely the washout it exists to prevent.

Where this is still approximation

Two places, and they're worth separating because only one of them is a compromise.

The first: there is one optical sky texture here and no infrared, microwave or radio sky to look up. So the mechanism is right — when each band stands in the visible, how much structure survives, what colour it carries — and the content is approximate. The real infrared sky is not a washed-out version of the optical one. That's a smaller lie than procedural folds, but it is one.

The second: exposure. At the very end the true brightness runs away by a factor of order a hundred thousand, and an eye pointed at that would simply see white with no information in it. The uniform part of that gain is divided back out so the sequence stays readable, which is what adaptation does anyway. Contrast within the frame is untouched — that comes from the real variation of g across the sky, and the cone's rim is still the brightest place in the image because that is where g genuinely diverges.

What's gone entirely is the golden ring. It sat across a quarter of the screen and implied it was a light source. It isn't one: this fall never reaches it, and a singularity has no surface, no temperature and nothing to emit with. There is not one photon in this exhibit that came from the ring. The code can still draw it as a thin marked contour — the way an imaging system outlines a structure that doesn't glow on its own — but that's an annotation, and it's off by default.

07

Where the code gives up, and why that's the interesting part

The integration stops at r = 0.55 M. That number was found rather than chosen — it's where the RK4 stepper stopped being able to hold the fall together, discovered by watching it fail. Here's the part worth pausing on: the inner horizon is at 0.4732 M. The code gives up 16% outside the surface where the physics gives up, without having been told about it.

That's not a coincidence, and it isn't a coordinate accident either. Both limits come from the same divergence. It just showed up in the arithmetic before anyone went looking for it in the theory.

How you tell a bug from a boundary

A conserved quantity that drifts is normally an accusation. H should sit at exactly −½ for the whole journey, and if it doesn't, the integrator is wrong. Watching the readout as the fall goes in:

|H + ½|
r = 1.933 M — first exceeds 10⁻⁷
r = 0.764 M — first exceeds 10⁻⁶  (the readout turns amber here)
r = 0.600 M — first exceeds 10⁻⁵
r = 0.550 M — H = −0.4999865, and the integration halts

So far that reads like ordinary accumulated error, and there's a standard way to check: refine the step and see whether the error shrinks. Halve the step size, and honest numerical error roughly halves with it.

Run at four resolutions and the peak drift comes out as 1.42, 1.60, 1.61, 1.62 × 10⁻⁵. It doesn't halve. It doesn't shrink at all — it converges onto a floor and sits there. Sixty-four thousand steps buys exactly nothing over eight thousand.

That's the tell. Numerical error goes to zero as you refine. This doesn't, because there's nothing left to refine toward. The equations are running out of things to say, and no amount of arithmetic recovers an answer that was never determined. The drift is on screen, live, in the bottom bar — it's the only readout on the site that shows a failure rather than a measurement.

There's a second, quieter limit worth knowing about. Kerr-Schild coordinates can only represent r ≥ 0 by construction. The negative-r region beyond the ring lives on a different sheet of the solution that these coordinates simply don't cover. So even with a perfect integrator, and even if the inner horizon were stable, this coordinate system could not go there. Two independent reasons, same answer.

What the ending actually shows

The last thing you see is a blueshift running away, and it is mostly the real one. Every pixel already carries a shift factor g computed from its photon's conserved energy — that's what colours the whole interior. The rim of the dark cone is where the energy measured at infinity passes through zero, so g runs toward infinity there, making it the brightest edge on the sky by a wide margin.

The final sequence amplifies g rather than painting over it, which means the burn-out starts at that rim and spreads outward in the order the geometry dictates, not in the order that looked good. The last few seconds — the flat white, and the fade after it — are a decision. The screen goes white because there's nothing left to compute, and staying on a frozen frame would have implied otherwise.

So the split, drawn where the physics draws it:

Computed, per pixel, no shortcuts. The camera's entire path. Every photon path, outside the horizon and inside it, from the observer's own tetrad. The edge of the dark cone, which is not drawn and not tuned — it's the locus where the traced photon's conserved energy changes sign, evaluated per pixel. The relativistic aberration and the redshift of the sky, both emerging from the local frame rather than applied on top of it. The accretion disk's Doppler beaming and gravitational redshift, folded into a single factor. The horizon positions, the ISCO, the ring's radius. The Hamiltonian drift in the readout.

Drawn. Very little, now. The interior filaments and the ring's glow are gone — they used to fill the part of the sky where the physics correctly returns nothing, and the honest answer there was to return nothing. What's left is the colour mapping from shift factor to screen colour, the last few frames of white, and the fade. The ring contour still exists in the code as an optional annotation and ships switched off.

Adjusted. Time, throughout: the real fall takes about twenty minutes of proper time and this shows it in under a minute, unevenly, slowing down for the parts worth looking at. And the redshift, which properly dims the sky as the fourth power of the shift factor — applied honestly, the interior would be a black screen with a rumour of a window in it. The exponent here is turned down to a little over one, which keeps the colour shift visible and the brightness watchable.

One last thing worth saying plainly. The observer in this exhibit is a fantasy: something that holds itself together through tidal forces that would destroy anything real. For Sagittarius A* that fantasy costs almost nothing at the horizon, where the stretch across a human body is about a ten-thousandth of Earth gravity and you would genuinely feel nothing. A stellar-mass black hole would be a different story entirely: tidal force at the horizon scales as 1/M², so a ten-solar-mass hole pulls on you roughly a hundred billion times harder there. You'd be shredded long before you had anything to look at.

And the thing this exhibit no longer does: it doesn't take you anywhere afterwards. There was a version that did, and it was better television. This one ends where the theory ends, which is a stranger and more honest place to be left standing.

08

Two bugs that were invisible in the code and obvious on the screen

Both of these were reported the same way: it stutters, and it cooks the GPU. Neither turned out to be where you'd look first, so they're worth writing down.

The fall kept stopping and starting

Somewhere around the horizon the camera would slow almost to a halt, jerk, and pick back up again. The obvious suspects — dropped frames, garbage collection, the trajectory table — were all wrong. The trajectory is fine: probed over 1962 samples, the radius is strictly decreasing, zero outward steps, so playback never has anything to stall on.

The cause was the pacing curve. The fall used to be spliced together from three separately eased segments — approach, crossing, interior — and each one matched its neighbour in value. Position was continuous, nothing looked wrong in review. But the derivative did not match, and the derivative is the thing your eye actually sees:

dv/dt across the seams
t = 21.5 s — 0.00276  approach easing out towards zero
t = 22.0 s — 0.00946  crossing begins at constant speed  ← jump
t = 26.0 s — 0.00304  interior easing begins at zero  ← 3× drop
t = 27.5 s — 0.01216  and recovers

Two velocity discontinuities, both sitting inside the horizon-crossing window — which is precisely the stretch this exhibit spends its whole argument insisting is uneventful.

The fix is structural rather than a tuned constant. Instead of splicing eased positions together, define a speed profile that is smooth and strictly positive, then integrate it to get position. Continuity of both is then guaranteed by construction. The profile eases up over the first 14% of the fall, holds flat, and eases down to rest over the last 20% — built from smootherstep, so speed is C² and progress is C³. Measured across the new curve, dv/dt is a flat 0.0302 through the entire crossing region, with no seam left to find.

The flat middle is also the more honest choice. Progress is linear in log r, which is roughly linear in how much bigger the hole looks now, so a constant rate is close to a constant rate of visible change. The old curve slowed down at the horizon, which is exactly the wrong place for an exhibit whose entire claim is that the horizon is not a place.

Twenty-eight metric evaluations per step, where four would do

The other complaint was heat. A high-end card was sitting at 68 °C rendering a black hole, which is not a reasonable price for this.

Two things were wrong. The smaller one first: there was no frame cap. On a 165 or 240 Hz display an uncapped loop renders three or four times the frames anyone can see, at full power draw, for the whole duration. Capping at 60 is invisible and removes most of the excess directly.

The larger one was in the integrator. Hamilton's equations need ∂H/∂x, and that was being computed as a central difference — six evaluations of H per derivative, four derivatives per RK4 step. Twenty-eight evaluations of the metric per step, each with a square root inside it, for up to 900 steps per pixel. It was the single most expensive thing in the shader by a wide margin.

It didn't need to be. H is quadratic in momentum and everything spatial sits in two places, so the gradient closes in form:

−∂H/∂x = ½ S² ∇f + f S ∇(l·p)
S = l·p − p_t
∇r = (r³x, r³y, r z (r²+a²)) / D,  from differentiating r⁴ − (ρ²−a²)r² − a²z² = 0
D = r⁴ + a²z², the same denominator f already uses

Both gradients reduce to ∇r plus bookkeeping, so one metric evaluation now serves an entire derivative: four per step instead of twenty-eight.

Checked before it went anywhere near the shader. Against a refined difference the closed form agrees to about one part in 10⁵ — which is the difference's own truncation error, not ours. Then the real test: trace 1500 photons per camera position through both versions and compare where they end up. Identical outcomes in every case, worst direction disagreement 0.0125°, and the peak Hamiltonian error along the ray came out lower with the closed form. So it is faster and slightly more correct, which is a nicer result than it usually is.

The ending was still being asserted

This one only showed up because the whole ray setup got ported to the CPU to check something else. With the camera parked where the trajectory leaves it, you can compute the shift factor for every direction on the sky without a GPU — and then just read off which spectral band each pixel lands in as the finale runs.

It did not land where the previous chapter says it should. The median pixel topped out at g = 197: far-infrared, deep amber. The microwave background needs g ≈ 2000. Only 39% of the frame ever reached the daylight band, which means the white ending was still coming from the safety wash — the asserted ending this whole rewrite exists to remove, still there, just better disguised.

The cause was a units mistake, in a sense. The cascade is logarithmic in g — g ≈ 2 is blue, 10 near-infrared, 100 far-infrared, 2000 microwave — but the amplification ramp was linear. A linear ramp across a logarithmic quantity spends its entire range inside the top decade and never traverses the rest:

linear 1 + 320·b  vs  exponential 103.5b
b = 0.1 — g = 20 near-IR  ·  g = 1.4 optical
b = 0.3 — g = 60 far-IR  ·  g = 6.9 near-IR
b = 0.5 — g = 99 far-IR  ·  g = 34 far-IR
b = 0.85 — g = 168 far-IR  ·  g = 580 microwave
b = 1.0 — g = 197 far-IR  ·  g = 1942 microwave

The second problem was brightness, and it had been hidden behind the first. The shader divides part of the amplification back out so the finale brightens gradually instead of saturating in one frame — but with the exponent it was using, and a sky that has been prefiltered down to its mean value, the median pixel came out of the tonemapper at 0.67 on a scale where white is about 1.7. Dim amber. Traced through the tonemapper properly, the exponent that saturates on its own is 1.25, not 1.6.

With both fixed the finale walks the whole cascade — optical, blue, near-IR, far-IR, daylight — over its eight seconds, and reaches white without the wash. The ordering is unchanged and still set by the geometry: the rim of the dark cone, where the shift factor is twelve times the median, saturates roughly halfway through and the burn-out spreads outward from there.

And nobody could see any of it

The same probe answered a question that had been sitting unresolved: whether the cascade was working at all. It could not be checked from a screenshot, because pointing the camera in the fall direction shows the dark cone, which is black whether the cascade runs or not.

It turned out to be worse than that. At the end of the fall the shadow covers everything ahead — sweeping the camera and tracing the screen, the fraction with any source at all goes 0% at yaw 0, 3% at 45°, 47% at 90°, and only reaches 100% past 150°. So a visitor who never turns around watches the entire ending on an empty screen.

That is not wrong physics. Nothing does reach you from ahead, and the exhibit should not pretend otherwise by swinging the camera around on its own — that behaviour was removed for good reasons and is not coming back. The signal goes where every other signal in this exhibit goes: to the instruments. The Look back button lights up when there is genuinely nothing ahead, and the visitor decides.

Detecting "nothing ahead" is harder than it sounds, and two cheaper tests failed before the expensive one worked. Testing the dark cone is the wrong quantity — the cone is where light cannot exist, but the shadow, rays that carry positive energy and still fall in, is much wider, and it is the shadow you see. Testing against a measured shadow half-angle failed too, agreeing with a traced sweep 19 times out of 28, because the Kerr shadow is not axisymmetric: frame dragging makes it D-shaped, so no single half-angle describes it. Tabulated, the angle to the outward axis does not separate the two cases at all — at the end of the fall, looking straight ahead is dark at 140° from it while turning 60° gives you sky at 150°.

So it is measured by tracing: fifteen rays on a coarse grid, three times a second. Against a full traced sweep over five depths, eight yaw angles and two pitch angles, the shipped version agrees 40 times out of 40, at 1.4 ms per evaluation — about half a percent of one core. The cost is a second copy of the integrator, in JavaScript, that has to stay in step with the GLSL one. That is a real liability, and it is why the two cheaper tests were tried first.

Worth naming the part that was hiding. The old difference used a fixed step of 0.01 in position, which is fine out at r = 30 M and increasingly not fine as you fall — at the closest approach the geometry varies enough across that step that the estimate was off by 4.4%. It never showed up as a visible artefact, because it degraded gradually and everywhere at once. The speedup was the reason to do this work; the accuracy was the part that turned out to matter.

09

The bugs you only get on somebody else's GPU

This exhibit was written on one machine and is meant to run on a room full of them. That gap has its own class of bug: things that are correct on the desktop you tested and quietly wrong on a phone, a laptop with an integrated GPU, or an Apple chip. This chapter is a review pass looking specifically for those, plus one suggestion that turned out to be wrong and is worth writing down anyway.

A grey line down the sky, from a function that takes the wrong argument

The sky is sampled at an explicit level of detail, because a pixel that has wound twice around the hole covers hundreds of times more sky than it did in flat space and a point sample there is simply the wrong integral — chapter 06. That was being done with texture2D(sky, uv, lod), and the third argument of that function is not a level. It is a bias, added on top of whatever the hardware worked out from how much uv changes between neighbouring pixels.

Usually that is a small correction. At one place it is not. The sky is an equirectangular map, so u = atan2(z, x)/2π + 0.5, and that wraps from 1 back to 0 at the anti-meridian. Two adjacent pixels straddling it differ by nearly a whole texture width, the hardware concludes the pixel covers the entire sky, and asks for log₂(6000) ≈ 12.5 — the top of the mip chain, which is the average colour of everything. One pixel wide, the full height of wherever that seam lands, a flat grey column.

The fix is the function that means what it says. textureLod() takes an absolute level, and it exists in GLSL ES 3.0, so the shader moved to WebGL2's shading language to get it. The number the shader computes is now the number the sampler uses.

That does throw away something real, though. The hardware's estimate is bad at the seam but it is the only part of the footprint that is measured per pixel rather than inferred from winding number, so it comes back — taken from how much the escape direction changes between neighbouring pixels instead of how much the texture coordinate does. Directions have no seam.

A random number that isn't random on half the hardware

Step lengths are dithered by about 5% per pixel so the integrator's own stepping does not print a pattern into the image. The dither came from fract(sin(dot(p, k)) · 43758.5453), which is the hash everybody uses and which is not portable. It works by taking the digits below the accuracy that sin is required to have, and how many of those there are, and what they look like, is up to the implementation. Apple's GPUs and several mobile parts are the usual places it goes wrong.

What that produces here is milder than it would be in a shading term — the failure is neighbouring pixels getting the same dither, so the dither stops working rather than drawing something of its own. Which is exactly the kind of failure you never see on the machine you develop on. It is a PCG hash on the integer pixel coordinate now: same instructions, same answer, every GPU.

Seven hundred arithmetic operations in the worst possible place

The accretion disk's turbulence was procedural: value noise, five octaves, four hash lookups per octave, and two cross-faded copies so it never visibly loops. That is 40 hash evaluations, about 770 arithmetic operations, every time a ray crosses the equatorial plane — and it was sitting inside the raymarching loop, which is the most expensive real estate in the shader. A ray that winds around the hole crosses two or three times.

None of it needs to be computed at runtime. Value noise on an integer lattice is tileable if you take the hash modulo the lattice period, so the four octaves are baked at startup into the four channels of one 512×512 texture at 8, 16, 32 and 64 cells across the tile. They share a texture coordinate, so one fetch returns all four and the whole thing collapses to a fetch and a dot product.

Two things had to change to make it tileable, and both were measured rather than eyeballed. Octaves have to be an integer number of cells, so the frequency ratio went from 2.1 to 2.0 and the octaves are decorrelated by seed instead. The fifth octave, carrying amplitude 1/32, is gone. Sampled 200,000 times over the disk's actual domain:

procedural vs baked, over r ∈ [ISCO, 5.5 Rs]
procedural — mean 0.4825, sd 0.1234
baked — mean 0.4837, sd 0.1204
shipped — one multiply-add closes the gap exactly

The other thing worth checking is whether the tile shows. A repeating texture read along a circle will print its period into the image if the circle closes on the tile. Measuring the angular autocorrelation of the disk pattern at four radii, looking for that peak: the baked version peaks between 0.17 and 0.38, the procedural one between 0.21 and 0.32. Same range, so what is being measured is finite-sample noise in both cases and not a repeat. A ring of radius ~9 in noise space does not close on an 8-unit torus.

There is a bonus in it. Procedural noise has no filtering at all, which is why the outer disk shimmers under motion. A texture has a mip chain, so the disk can now be sampled at a level that matches how much of the screen a turbulence cell actually covers — estimated from the distance the ray has travelled. It has to be an explicit level for the same reason as the sky, and a more serious one: an implicit level inside divergent control flow is undefined behaviour, and by the time a ray reaches the disk the four pixels of a quad are on different iterations of the loop.

One number where being wrong means a black screen

The metric carries D = r⁴ + a²z², which at the escape radius is 6.6 × 10⁸. The largest finite value in mediump is 65504. A fragment shader that fell back to medium precision would not look slightly worse; it would produce NaN for every ray more than about 16 units out, with no error anywhere, and you would spend an afternoon looking at the integrator. Every WebGL2 implementation is required to offer high precision in fragment shaders, so this should never happen — the check is there because of what it would look like if it did.

The suggestion that didn't survive the arithmetic

The most attractive proposal in the review was to replace the fixed-order RK4 integrator with an adaptive, error-controlled one — RKF45 or similar — on the reasoning that letting the step follow the curvature would cut the average step count by half or more. It is the right instinct and it does not work here, for three reasons that only show up when you count.

The first is that the step is already adaptive. It is proportional to radius, clamped, and tightened by a factor of four through the band around the photon sphere where rays wind — that tightening is measured, not guessed; at K = 2 a leak of rays escaping from inside the shadow disappears, and K = 3 leaves margin. What it is not is error-controlled, and error control is the expensive part rather than the free part.

The second is stage count. RK4 takes four derivative evaluations per step, and since the closed-form gradient landed each of those is one metric evaluation. An embedded 4/5 pair takes six. That is 50% more work per step before any error estimate is computed, so the step count would have to fall by a third just to break even — and rejected steps are paid for in full.

The third is the one that turns the argument around. The review's own diagnosis was register pressure: too many live values per thread means fewer threads in flight. An embedded pair holds six stage vectors live instead of four, plus the error norm and the retry state. It makes the thing it was proposed to fix worse.

The diagnosis underneath was still right. Threads do run in groups, and one ray grazing the photon sphere that needs 819 steps does hold up the other 63 in its group even though they resolved at 50. But that cost is in the geometry, not the integrator: those steps are genuinely needed, and no integration scheme makes that ray cheap. What is actually available is a ceiling — and the ceiling is now compiled per quality preset, so the eco modes build a 420 or 280 iteration loop rather than a 900 iteration one they will never finish.

Worth being straight about what has and has not been established here. The seam, the precision floor and the undefined-behaviour texture fetch are correctness bugs, and they are fixed. The noise statistics and the autocorrelation are measured. The instruction count is arithmetic. But none of it has been timed on the hardware it is meant to help — that needs a phone and a frame counter, not a spreadsheet, and until then the honest claim is that the shader now does much less work, not that it runs faster by some particular amount.

10

The failures that have nothing to do with the physics

Everything above is about getting the picture right. This chapter is about the picture not being there at all: the handful of ways a page that runs correctly on a desk can end up showing a black rectangle in a room, and what the code does about each of them. It also records a review finding that was described accurately and diagnosed wrongly, because that turned out to be the more useful thing to write down.

The leak that needed somewhere to leak into

A code review flagged this file for a textbook Three.js memory leak. Nothing calls .dispose() on the geometry, the material or the two textures. Nothing calls removeEventListener. There is a debug reference parked on window. GPU memory is not managed by the JavaScript garbage collector, so a 6000×3000 sky texture with its full mip chain stays resident until something explicitly releases it, and that is around 96 MB sitting in video memory with a live reference chain leading back to it.

Every sentence of that is a true description of the code. It also did not leak anything, and the reason is worth stating plainly because it is the sort of thing a general rule cannot know. A leak needs something to accumulate against. This exhibit is a document: index.html loads the module once, with a plain script tag, and the only way out of the page is a navigation. A navigation destroys the context, the canvas, the module, the listeners and the 96 MB together. There is no unmount, no route change, no second instance, so there is never a second copy to sit beside the first. The advice is right for a component in a single-page application and this is not one.

The teardown went in anyway, for a different reason, and it is worth separating the two because they lead to different code. A browser allows a limited number of live WebGL contexts per process — around sixteen in Chrome — and quietly kills the oldest when a new one asks for a slot. Pages held in the back/forward cache keep theirs. A visitor walking through five exhibits and then pressing back twice is precisely the traffic that spends that budget, and the symptom is not a slow leak, it is a context-lost event on the page somebody is currently looking at. Releasing the context on a real unload keeps the count honest. Releasing it on a back/forward-cache suspend would break the back button, so the handler checks which of the two it is.

Context loss, which is the one that actually happens

A driver reset, a laptop switching between integrated and discrete graphics, a phone throttling, a machine waking from sleep, or the context budget above: the browser takes the GPU context away from a page that is otherwise running perfectly. Without a handler the canvas goes black and never comes back. There is no error in the console and the rest of the page keeps working, so on a kiosk it reads as the exhibit having finished, and nobody notices until closing time.

One line is load-bearing. The webglcontextlost event has to be cancelled with preventDefault(), and if it is not, the browser treats the notification as advisory and never fires a restore event at all. Three.js does that itself and re-initialises its GL state on restore, re-uploading textures and geometry lazily, so most of the recovery is free. What it cannot know about is the wall-clock timer driving the fall. That has been running the whole time the screen was dead, and if the accumulated delta is handed to the timeline in one piece, a fifteen-second driver reset arrives as a fifteen-second jump down the trajectory. The clock is drained every frame while the context is gone.

A white screen from a missing file

If the sky texture fails to load, Three binds its one-pixel white placeholder in its place. That is a sensible default for a material and a bad one here: every ray that escapes to the sky returns maximum energy in all three channels, the exposure has nothing to work with, and the exhibit becomes a white rectangle. No error, and nothing in the image suggesting the cause is a 404 rather than something in the shader.

There is a fallback sky now, generated in about ten milliseconds if the load fails: twelve thousand points on a dark background, placed uniform in cos θ rather than uniform in texture coordinate, since equirectangular mapping stretches hardest at the poles and equal spacing in v would pile stars up exactly where each texel covers the least sky. It is not the NASA map and it says so on screen. The lensing, the aberration and the spectral cascade all still read against it, which is the part that matters.

One constant that was being asserted instead of measured

The prefiltering in chapter 06 needs to know how wide the sky texture is, because log₂ of that width is the top of the mip chain and the ceiling on what the shader may ask for. That number was written into a uniform by hand as 6000, four lines away from a filename ending in _4k. One of those two was wrong and nothing in the code could tell you which.

ceiling = log₂(width)
if 6000 — 12.55
if 4096 — 12.00
difference — half a mip level, at the end of the cascade

Half a level does not sound like much and it lands in the worst place: the final decades of the blueshift, where the whole point is that the sky goes flat because the source genuinely is flat rather than because the sampler ran out of chain. It is read off the decoded image now, in the load callback. Whatever is actually on disk is the ceiling, and there is nothing left to keep in sync.

Two things a room needs that a desk does not

The fall ends on a text plate and used to stay there. That is right for the web, where someone is reading six paragraphs and restarting under them at paragraph four is worse than leaving it up indefinitely. It is wrong for a gallery, where the same behaviour means an afternoon of showing a wall of text to an empty room. The loop is a URL flag rather than a build, and the timeout runs from the last time anybody touched anything rather than from when the plate appeared, so it cannot interrupt a visitor who is still there.

The smaller one: restarting reset the clock but not the heading. The camera kept whatever direction the previous visitor had left it pointing, and since the interesting half of the ending is behind you, a good fraction of second runs began facing backwards out of a hole still sixty Schwarzschild radii away. It resets both the target and the smoothed value, so it snaps rather than swinging through 180 degrees while the fall is already under way.

None of this is physics and all of it is the difference between an exhibit and a demo. The useful lesson is in the first section: the review was right about every observable fact and wrong about the conclusion, because it recognised a pattern without checking whether the pattern's precondition held. The fix that came out of it is real, and it is a fix for a different problem than the one that was reported.
